# How a trained fighter moves (reference for UNEDITED ep 1 fight)

Researched 2026-10-11. Strong sources = measured studies; weaker = coaching sites. Marked (S) study, (C) coaching.

## The big idea: power travels up the body, in order
- Force starts at the floor, goes legs -> hips -> trunk -> shoulder -> arm -> fist, each segment peaking just after the one below it (proximal-to-distal). Elite boxers show cleaner sequencing than juniors (S). A beginner swings the arm alone; a pro's arm is the last thing to move.
- Rear-leg drive is central to a punch's power and to staying strong when tired (S, 2025 force-plate study of 10 amateur boxers). The cross produced the highest force.
- So every strike: (1) weight sinks and loads the back leg, (2) back heel/foot pivots, knee turns in, (3) hips rotate, (4) shoulders follow, (5) arm extends last, fist lands as the body weight arrives, (6) snaps back to guard.

## Stance (before and after every move)
- Bladed (side-on), feet about shoulder width, knees soft, weight slightly forward on the balls of the feet, heels light. Chin tucked behind the lead shoulder, both hands high near the face, elbows in. Never stands flat-footed, square, or with straight legs.
- Constant small movement (bounce, weight shifts) rather than freezing. Head never stays on one line.

## Punches (C unless marked)
- Jab: lead hand, fast, little hip turn (about 45-60 deg), small step in with the lead foot. It is a measuring and setup tool, not a power shot.
- Cross: rear hand, most rotation (about 120-160 deg of hip/shoulder turn), rear foot pivots on the ball, rear heel lifts and turns out, shoulder comes up to protect the chin, lead hand stays up. Highest force (S).
- Hook: lead foot pivots, hips and trunk whip around, elbow stays high (about 90 deg), fist travels in a short horizontal arc. Hooks reach the highest hand speed (S, 2011 study).
- Uppercut: legs dip, then drive up, rotating up through the trunk.
- After every punch the hand returns to the face along the same line; the head moves off line (slip, angle step) so the fighter is not where the counter lands.
- Combinations flow: jab-cross-hook, the second punch starts as the first ends, hips alternate left/right.

## Kicks
- Roundhouse (S, 2017 study of expert kickers): rapid pelvic rotation, hip abduction and flexion, then fast knee extension; the body's center of mass moves toward the target. Supporting foot pivots (heel turns toward the opponent, about 80 deg of hip rotation), arm on the kicking side swings down/back for balance, the other hand covers the head. Phases: chamber (knee up and out) -> swing -> extension and contact with the shin -> recoil back to stance.
- Teep / push kick (C): rear or lead knee drives up to about 90 deg chamber, hips thrust forward as the leg extends straight at the body, small pivot on the standing foot, hands stay up, torso stays tall (leaning back makes it weak and slow), fast retract.
- Front kick: no swing arc, hip flexion plus knee extension straight at the target.

## Defense
- Slip: head moves a few inches off the punch line, to the outside of a straight punch, feet stay planted, knees bend a little (not the waist), hands stay up, then counter.
- Duck/bob and weave: bend the knees to drop the head under a hook, travel in a U shape, come up on the other side of the arm.
- Roll (shoulder roll): rotate the torso so the punch glances off the shoulder, best against hooks; dangerous if you roll the wrong way.
- Parry: small sweep of the glove or forearm to redirect a straight punch, then counter. Not good against combinations.
- Footwork: pivot on the lead foot to leave the line of attack and land at an angle; circle; step back out of range. Without footwork the head movement does not work.
- Layers: slip/roll sets up the counter, footwork makes the angle.

## Animation principles that make it read as a fight (C)
- Anticipation sets the weight: a small sink and load before the strike. A giant or a heavy hit gets a longer, bigger wind-up (the viewer needs time to read it).
- Overshoot ("snap"): the strike goes slightly past the target and settles back; this reads as impact.
- Hit reactions sell the hit more than the attacker does: head snaps, spine curves away, weight transfers, a beat of freeze (hitstop, 2-3 frames) on contact.
- Follow-through on the early hits; consistent timing keeps strikes quick but readable.
- Staging: alternate action and reaction; reactions can be dodges, blocks, parries, not only hits.
- Phases of every attack: startup (wind-up), active (contact), recovery (return to guard). Recovery is where a fighter is vulnerable - a pro recovers fast, a brute (the KillDroid) recovers slowly and gets punished.

## Who fights how in ep 1
- Relic (AI-driven body, trained combat library): economical pro mechanics - bladed stance, tight guard, slips and angle steps, counters on recovery, kinetic-chain punches and teeps. Starts rusty (100 years buried, 3% battery), so early moves are late and stiff, then the AI finds the rhythm.
- KillDroid: a heavy brute. Huge arcing swings (claw slash, fist hammer), long wind-ups, planted feet, slow recovery, but one hit nearly ends Relic. Few pro habits: no guard, no head movement.
